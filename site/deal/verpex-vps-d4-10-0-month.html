Before you order: three things worth checking yourself

  • Run a latency and packet-loss check against the provider's test IP from where your actual visitors are — not from where you are. A route that looks fine from your desk can collapse on a different carrier.
  • Re-check the same measurement during the provider's evening peak. Averages taken at midday hide exactly the congestion you are trying to avoid.
  • Read the renewal price, not just the first-term price, and add up two years of it. A headline number that only applies to the first billing cycle is not the number you will be paying.

What actually matters when comparing these plans

  • Decide what the server is for before comparing specs. A box that only runs a scheduled job has completely different needs from one serving pages to visitors.
  • Check the traffic allowance and what overage costs, before you check core count. Running out of monthly transfer is a harder failure than running out of CPU.
  • Confirm whether the provider charges separately for a public IPv4 address, snapshots, or a data disk. These routinely change the real monthly cost.
  • Verify the upgrade, downgrade, migration and refund rules in writing. Being able to upgrade does not imply you can downgrade later.
  • Treat a snapshot as a rollback convenience, not a backup. If the instance is destroyed and the snapshot goes with it, you did not have a backup.

Common traps in this price band

  • A low first-term price that reverts to a much higher renewal price is the single most common surprise. Record both numbers before you buy.
  • Peak bandwidth and sustained throughput are different things. A large port number can coexist with a shared link, a monthly cap, or throttling after you exceed the allowance.
  • Two plans with identical core and RAM counts can perform very differently depending on CPU scheduling, disk latency, and how loaded the neighbouring tenants are. A spec sheet does not settle this.
  • Cheap annual prepay is how providers lock in customers, not a favour to you. On a provider you have not used before, start with the shortest term available.

What this kind of server suits

  • Suitable: a personal website, an API endpoint, a webhook receiver, a scheduled automation job, or a light monitoring service.
  • Not suitable: bulk media storage, a NAS replacement, continuous video transcoding, or GPU inference. A general-purpose VPS is the wrong resource type for those, and no amount of extra cores fixes it.
